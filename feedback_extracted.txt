Strategic Items the Developer Should Hear

10. The simulation's competitive position

For corporate governance training, this simulation competes with case-method classroom
teaching (Harvard Business School style) and traditional e-learning platforms (LinkedIn
Learning, Coursera).

What this simulation does better than classroom case method:

     Personalised dynamic feedback at scale
     Repeatable practice with consistent rubric
     24/7 availability
     Quantitative scoring tied to specific rubric dimensions

What this simulation does worse than classroom case method:

     No peer learning or discussion
     Limited scenario variety (one company across all rounds)
     No real-world ambiguity (M6 vocabulary is the "right" answer)
     No nuanced ethical dilemmas requiring judgment over framework application

Strategic recommendation: Position this as a complement to classroom learning, not a
replacement. The strongest deployment is as homework/preparation before classroom case
discussion or as practice/reinforcement after.

11. Scaling considerations for institutional deployment

If your developer is planning to scale this for actual training cohorts (corporate clients,
professional certification programs, MBA programs), some specific considerations:

     Anti-cheating mechanisms: Currently the simulation appears to have no protection
         against players sharing optimal responses. For high-stakes assessment use,
         consider rotating scenarios per cohort.

     Cohort analytics: Aggregate scoring data across players would show the developer
         which M6 concepts are well-taught vs poorly-taught by the simulation. Currently
         there's no visible analytics layer.

     Instructor dashboard: For institutional deployment, instructors need visibility into
         their students' scores, common failure modes, and which scenarios produce which
         patterns of behaviour.

12. The cost structure of LLM-powered simulations
Each playthrough of this simulation likely involves substantial LLM API calls (5 rounds ×
multiple consultation prompts × dissenter generation × scoring evaluation × feedback
generation = potentially 50-100+ LLM calls per player). At scale, this is meaningfully
expensive.

Recommendation for developer: Track per-playthrough token costs. If costs exceed
sustainable per-user pricing, consider:

     Caching common dissenter responses
     Using smaller models for routine generation, larger models for scoring
     Pre-generating scenario content rather than real-time generation

13. The simulation is M6-bound but doesn't say so

A player encountering this simulation has no way to know that it's specifically testing Module
6 Session 1 content. There's no explicit framing. A new player might think they're being
tested on general corporate governance, AI ethics, crisis management, or financial analysis
broadly.

Recommendation: Add an opening screen that explicitly states:

     "This simulation tests your mastery of Module 6 Session 1: Understanding and
         Analysing Financial Ratios"

     "Specific concepts tested: Prudence, Accounting Equation, Components of Equity,
         Preference Share Capital, Asset Valuation under AS 36"

     "Your performance will be scored against M6 vocabulary deployment, governance
         discipline, and decision quality"

This anchors the player's expectations and improves educational outcomes by signalling
what to focus on.

15. The persuasion engine's character integrity is underutilised

Your feedback notes that character biases drive opposition correctly. What's missing is that

Recommendation for developer: Tune the persuasion engine so that character-specific
arguments have multiplied effect on character-specific dissenters. This rewards players who
tailor their arguments to the audience, which is a real boardroom skill.
